Eight sessions, measured. Run them on evidence.

Every Foundations session our Singapore chapter has run since August 2024 was recorded, transcribed and coded. This guide reports what each of the eight sessions actually produces in the room, which questions were put, who speaks, and which of the things convenors worry about turn out not to matter. Every recommendation carries the measurement behind it.

Before the eight

The shape of a session

The sequence is the one feature of a Foundations evening with a measurable effect, and it should be run whole. The chapter's own practice sets the scale: a mean of 98 minutes, from 75 to 131, with 8 to 27 voices in the room and four to eight questions put, a median of six.

Run of show

About 100 minutes

  1. 0:00Arrive and welcomeSay why the room exists. Keep it short.10 min
  2. 0:10Introduction roundEveryone speaks: name, business, what brought them, a fun fact.25 min
  3. 0:35FilmThe teaching segment. Median across recorded sessions is 23% of the evening.15 min
  4. 0:50DiscussionThree questions keyed to the film, tables of six to eight.40 min
  5. 1:30Commitment and closeOne action each before next month, then pray.10 min
Why the order matters

The two segments do different work

They are not interchangeable. Introduction rounds are where calling and the church come up; discussion is where ethics, identity, risk and suffering rise. Across the corpus, calling appears in 49% of introduction turns and falls to 23 in discussion, while ethics and excellence rise from 4% to 34, Spirit-guidance from 10 to 34, identity from 8 to 29, and both risk and suffering from 6 to 21.

An introduction round cannot do the work of a discussion, and a discussion without one starts cold. In all 24 recorded sessions the discussion ran deeper than the round preceding it.

The comparison is between segments of a fixed format rather than between conditions, so it shows the sequence doing what it is designed to do. It does not show that an alternative format would do less.

What the room says

The room holds more theology than it chooses to use

These are findings about the theology itself rather than about how to run an evening. They are the reason the protocol below is worth trusting.

83 against 40 against 9

Guidance, not gain

Of 181 Pentecostal markers, 83 name the Spirit's direction and 40 expect material blessing. Only 9 name the work itself as something the Spirit gifts, voiced by nine different people, so no one develops the thought twice. Prosperity organises 3.6% of all talk. The room is fluent about being led and nearly mute about being provided for.

37 / 25 / 21 / 17%

Four traditions in one room

Weighted doctrinal material runs Evangelical-Protestant at 37%, Pentecostal-Charismatic at 25, Roman Catholic at 21, and a cross-traditional ascetic register at 17. The repertoire available to the room is far wider than the part it uses.

158 of 618 contributions

The market, not the ancestors

Where the room reaches for a cultural script it reaches for a migrant and mercantile one: expatriate and transnational life 44 times, global mission 36, the multi-faith workplace 27. Ancestral obligation barely surfaces, though the coding has a category for it. And where the room takes a position on these scripts, it contests them more often than it affirms them.

9 of 28 against a baseline of 14

Idiom does not identify the congregation

For the 28 speakers whose church could be verified independently, the tradition they sounded like matched their actual congregation 9 times. Always naming the room's commonest tradition would have scored 14. Doctrinal accent is not a membership card, and you cannot infer someone's church from how they pray.

Sex and trade, not church

The people predict the theology

The attributes that predict which register a believer uses are the believer's own: sex and daily trade. The formal church, the film shown and the room sat in predict nothing the coding could detect. The theological selection is made by the people present, not by the institutional setting.

What the evidence says

Seven findings that should change how you run it

Each carries the measurement behind it. Where a result is suggestive rather than established, it says so.

24 of 24 sessions

The question does the work

Theology arrives the moment the room is asked something it cannot answer from a CV, and it does not build through the evening. In every one of the 24 recorded sessions the discussion carried more doctrinal markers per contribution than the introduction round before it, 1.77 against 0.66 (paired Wilcoxon p = 1.2 x 10^-7). The rise is a step at the boundary between the two segments, not a slope: once the discussion starts, how late a person speaks tells you nothing about how deeply they speak (median rho = -0.01, p = 0.66).

DoKeep the introduction round and keep it short, then put a question tied to the teaching. A fellowship with no second question has asked only the one whose answer is a business card.

rho = -0.55, q = 0.020

Split rooms above sixteen

More voices meant fewer turns each, with no gain in depth. Recorded rooms ran from 8 to 27 voices.

DoTables of six to eight, each with a briefed host.

14% to 60%

Talk less than you think

Facilitator share of turns varied fourfold across recorded sessions. Higher share went with fewer participant turns (rho = -0.46, p = 0.027) and no more depth (rho = +0.01). The correlation does not survive multiple-comparison correction.

DoAim under a third of the turns, and treat this as a direction rather than a proven effect.

p = 0.20

Do not agonise over the film

Across 15 standard and 9 substituted sessions, marker density was indistinguishable (1.89 against 1.58, Mann-Whitney p = 0.20). What a substitute film changes is the subject matter, not the quality of the conversation.

DoPick the film for the conversation you want, then write questions about the film you actually showed.

224 arrangements

Meet wherever is convenient

Three measures appeared to separate sessions held in worship buildings from those held elsewhere, at q = 0.069. All three collapse under an exact permutation test that holds cohort fixed.

DoThe room does not change what gets said. Choose for parking and quiet.

q = 0.013 to 0.046

Expect the room to divide by sex

Women's contributions carry Pentecostal-charismatic markers at 30.4% of their weighted marker mass against 21.1 for men, and raise Spirit-guidance and prayer at 30.1 against 18.3. Men raise wealth and stewardship at 24.3 against 13.8. This is the most robust contrast in the corpus, and it is an emphasis rather than a partition: of the 146 people who voice either register, 49 voice both.

DoMove the addressee, not the topic. When the conversation turns to money, put the question to the women first; when it turns to guidance and prayer, put it to the men.

37.9% against 17.7%

Read the trades in the room

What someone does all week shapes what they bring. Speakers working in finance and investment raise wealth and stewardship in 37.9% of their contributions against 17.7% for everyone else (q = 0.015, and it survives dropping any one cohort). Sector conditions the subject matter, though not the doctrinal register.

DoLook at the roster before choosing the question. A table of financiers will take you to stewardship whether you planned it or not.

Answers

Common questions

How long should a Faith Driven Entrepreneur Foundations session run?

Across 24 recorded sessions the mean length was 98 minutes, ranging from 75 to 131. Longer sessions produced more turns of conversation (Spearman rho = 0.64, p = 0.0007) but not deeper ones, so roughly 90 to 110 minutes is the working range.

Does the opening introduction round matter?

Yes. In 24 of 24 recorded sessions the discussion that followed was deeper than the introduction round preceding it, with doctrinal markers per contribution rising from 0.66 to 1.77. The two segments also elicit different subjects: calling and the church dominate introductions, while ethics, identity, risk and suffering rise in discussion.

How many people should be in the room?

More voices meant fewer turns each (rho = -0.55, q = 0.020) with no gain in depth. Recorded rooms ran from 8 to 27 voices. Above roughly sixteen, split into tables of six to eight.

Does it matter which film is shown?

Less than convenors assume. Across 15 sessions using the standard film and 9 using a substitute, marker density was statistically indistinguishable (1.89 against 1.58, Mann–Whitney p = 0.20). What the film changes is the subject matter, not the depth.

Does the venue change the conversation?

No effect survives testing. Three measures appeared to separate sessions held in worship buildings from those held elsewhere, but all three collapse once cohort is held fixed by an exact permutation test. Meet wherever is convenient.

How much should the facilitator talk?

Less. Facilitator share ranged from 14 to 60% of turns. Higher facilitator share was associated with fewer participant turns (rho = -0.46, p = 0.027), though this does not survive multiple-comparison correction. Treat it as a direction.

Do men and women contribute differently?

Yes, and it is the most robust contrast in the corpus. Women's turns carry Pentecostal-charismatic markers at 30.4% of their weighted marker mass against 21.1% for men (q = 0.013), and raise Spirit-guidance and prayer at 30.1% against 18.3 (q = 0.036). Men raise wealth and stewardship at 24.3% against 13.8 (q = 0.046).

How many questions should be asked in a session?

Between four and eight were put in recorded sessions, with a median of six. No recorded session got through more than about three at any one table. Choose three and drop the rest.

Where the numbers come from

Source. The Faith Driven Entrepreneur Foundations Singapore corpus: 24 sessions recorded between August 2024 and August 2026 across three cohorts, 2,349 minutes of video, 873 coded conversational turns of which 618 are participant speech from 289 distinct people. Every session was recorded and published as part of the chapter's ordinary practice.

How to read the figures. Depth is the number of doctrinal markers carried by a contribution, averaged over the contributions in a segment. Theme percentages are the share of participant contributions in that session carrying the subject, against the share across all 618 participant contributions. Facilitator share is the facilitator's proportion of turns. Subgroup contrasts use Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction within families; session-level associations use Spearman rank correlation over 24 points.

Reliability. Three large language models independently recoded a stratified sample of seventy-two contributions from the codebook alone. Cohen's κ against the original coding runs 0.67, 0.63 and 0.57 averaged over the four doctrinal families, and 0.73 to 0.84 on the Pentecostal family that carries the gender finding. The models mark more sparingly than the original coding, so the proportions here are if anything conservative. The stance scheme is unattested.

Limits. The marker scheme rests on a single coding pass, and the questions were written by the convenor, so the thematic distribution reflects what was asked as well as what the room believes. Session-level parameters rest on 24 points, so the negative findings are failures to detect rather than demonstrations that no effect exists. Gender is unresolved for 100 of 420 appearance records. This is one chapter in one city and is best understood as an information-rich case rather than a sample.
