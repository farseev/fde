What 22 sessions taught us: 7 insights from Singapore's faith-driven founders

We recorded, transcribed and coded two years of Faith Driven Singapore conversations. Here is what the data says Christ-following founders in Singapore care about.

Quick answer

Across 22 recorded Foundations sessions (August 2024 to August 2026, three cohorts, about 36 hours), Faith Driven Singapore coded 776 live sharings. Seven patterns stand out: (1) calling is the core theme; (2) founders care about hearing God far more than about prosperity; (3) the unicorn myth drives burnout; (4) faith rewrites Asian scripts about shame and money; (5) witness in a multi-faith city is character-first; (6) the room is ecumenical in practice; (7) a mobile, neutral community keeps people coming back.

How we did it

Every recorded session on our YouTube channel was transcribed the same way. Each live sharing was coded by question, theme (from a fixed list of 20) and signal, then checked back against its transcript. Curriculum films were excluded, and the facilitator's voice was kept separate, leaving 550 participant sharings. Participants came from at least 16 countries.

1. Calling is the centre of gravity

Calling and vocation is the most common theme: 187 of 550 participant sharings, about one in three. Founders keep coming back to why they build. A digital-studio founder described feeling "looked down upon" in Singapore for not taking a corporate job, until "Jesus showed up in my life: I created you for a reason."

2. Discernment, not prosperity

Spirit guidance and prayer comes second (127 sharings). Explicit prosperity talk appears only 19 times, and pushback against it slightly outnumbers support (11 to 9). The distinctly charismatic thread in these rooms isn't "God will make you rich". It is "God will tell you what to do": "Holy Spirit is my partner in this business… till You speak I will not do anything." (watch)

Some participants also asked honestly how you hear God. "In times of confusion it is hard to discern," one said, and suggested "we could have a class on that".

3. The unicorn myth is a mental-health issue

Identity and success (105) and suffering and mental health (79) rank high. A former venture investor described depression and burnout driven by VCs demanding "100x or at least 10x". A mentor of 17 years observed that "if you're absolutely completely normal you wouldn't be an entrepreneur… we're more vulnerable people." The counterweight in the room was theological: "God is no less God because my business has cratered." See Don't worship work.

4. Faith rewrites Asian success scripts

A quarter of all sharings (193 of 776) draw on a cultural script. The pattern is selective:

  • Critiqued every time: shame about failure. "Everybody thinks that to be a leader you must be the smartest, the strongest. It's actually not true. A leader must be authentic, vulnerable."
  • Mostly critiqued: Western hustle and VC culture, and the Chinese-family script that "money is the most important thing".
  • Negotiated: religious plurality at work.
  • Appreciated: Singapore's order, safety and just systems.

5. Witness in a multi-faith city is character-first

Most participants favoured earning the right to speak. They pray for a non-Christian client "as an outflow of doing things well", tell Bible stories to Muslim colleagues while knowing "I cannot go deeper", and hope people ask, "why are you like this? How come you have so much hope?" This is the Singapore version of FDE's Ministry in Word.

6. Ecumenical in practice

The room includes people from City Harvest, New Creation, Kingdomcity, Methodist and Lutheran churches, Catholics, house churches, two self-identified Orthodox Christians, and seekers who "haven't chosen my religion yet". Participants' language mixes evangelical, charismatic, Catholic social-teaching and Orthodox emphases, such as serving the common good, letting go of success, and treating brokenness as grace. It is ecumenism built on shared practice rather than on formal dialogue.

7. A mobile community keeps people

The group met in seven venues in two years, all within about 15 km of each other, and 23 of 24 sessions were in Singapore's Central Region. Even so, 47 people came back, 24 followed the group to more than one venue, and 16 crossed from one cohort to the next. Because Singapore is small, moving is cheap. Neutral venues, which belong to no single church, suit a room drawn from many churches. And founders say they need each other: "Entrepreneurs struggle a lot… that's why the community is very important."

What this means if you're a founder in Singapore

  • You are not strange for asking what God thinks about your business. It's the most common question in the room.
  • You're not alone in the burnout, the shame or the money pressure.
  • There is a room where you can talk about all of it without anyone selling to you. Come to the next gathering.

Frequently asked questions

What do Christian entrepreneurs in Singapore care about most?

In Faith Driven Singapore's coded sessions, the most common themes are calling and vocation, hearing God in decisions, stewardship of wealth, identity apart from success, and ethics and excellence.

Is the prosperity gospel common among Singapore's faith-driven founders?

Not in these groups. Prosperity language appeared in 19 of 550 participant sharings, and pushback against it slightly outnumbered support.

How was this data collected?

From 22 recorded Faith Driven Singapore Foundations sessions (2024–2026), transcribed with speech recognition, coded by theme and verified against the transcripts. Facilitator speech and curriculum films were separated out.
